01 about

I'm a first-year PhD student in Information Technology at George Mason University, advised by Dr. Marcos Zampieri. I work on applied AI and NLP, and I point it at two domains on purpose: education and healthcare. To me they are the two most impactful domains there are. Almost everyone passes through a classroom and a clinic, so a tool that works well in either one can play a positive role in a lot of people's lives. That is where I want my applied AI and NLP expertise to go.

Before Mason I spent five years at the University of Arizona, where I finished a BS and an MS in Computer Science (GPA 3.90). In my last year and a half there I worked in the Deep Target NLP Lab with Dr. Gondy Leroy, building a pipeline that pulls Valley Fever symptoms out of patient records without ever sending those records to an outside API. That work became my first paper as first author, which I presented at IEEE ICHI 2026 in Minneapolis.

The other half of my time in Tucson was spent teaching. Six semesters as a TA for intro Python and Java, a summer bootcamp on LLMs for people who had never written code, and a student AI club I co-founded that grew past a hundred members. A lot of my research questions come from watching people learn. These experiences naturally shaped my goal of pursuing a PhD and, one day, becoming a faculty member myself.

What drives me is a genuine interest in designing and implementing reliable AI and NLP systems for social good in impactful domains such as healthcare, education, and beyond.

03 research

Three threads, one habit: build the test set first, then find out what the model really does.

i.

Clinical NLP that stays inside the hospital

Valley Fever is common in Arizona and easy to miss, because its symptoms look like a cold or pneumonia. I built a pipeline that finds its symptoms and social determinants of health in EHR text. Instead of sending patient notes to a commercial LLM, we used an LLM to label synthetic sentences, then fine-tuned a small BERT model on those labels. I also put together a 1,200-sentence challenge set full of implicit symptoms, heavy jargon, and stacked negations, and compared clinical encoders (BioClinical ModernBERT) against small quantized local LLMs (MedGemma, Llama) that a hospital could realistically run.

with Dr. Gondy Leroy · University of Arizona

ii.

Does the model reason, or does it pattern-match?

Using the FLDx2 formal logic corpus, I designed controlled experiments around three questions. If a model can do a 3-step proof, can it do a 6-step one? Does it still work when you add facts that don't matter? And what happens when you slip a negation in? What we found: the models got more distracted as we added irrelevant premises to the context, and they struggled with proofs involving negations. (Negation, again. See thread i.)

with Dr. Liangming Pan · University of Arizona

iii.

LLMs and the intro CS classroom

Students in CS1 are already using LLMs, so the question is less should they and more how good are these models at the actual coursework? My first PhD project builds on CSEPrompts (Raihan et al., Journal of Intelligent Information Systems), a benchmark of intro CS prompts. I'm making it executable, so models get graded the way a student would, by running their code against tests, and stretching it across more human languages and more programming languages. A student in Dhaka or São Paulo asks the same questions as one in Fairfax, just not in English. As a Bangla speaker, that part is personal.

It also comes from the TA desk. As a CS TA before and after ChatGPT, I have seen how students rely on AI-generated code, and I keep thinking about how to design tools that leverage the benefits of GenAI without harming real learning.

ongoing · with Dr. Marcos Zampieri and Nishat Raihan · George Mason University
